CTI (Cyber Threat Intelligence) Write-up: Ransomware Leak Sites, a Payment Trace and AI Alerts

Nov 11, 2026 | Aaron M. Wood

One-page brief (PDF) →

The Idea

I wanted to gain real-world experience and to understand how ransomware groups operate in real situations. This included how often they post victims on leak sites, what methods they use, and whether it’s possible to reliably trace sanctioned funds on-chain. Over 11 days… from Oct 30 to Nov 9, 2026, I set my focus on three groups: Qilin and Akira (known as REVENANT SPIDER and PUNK SPIDER by CrowdStrike) and SafePay (which doesn’t have a public CrowdStrike name). I compared their reported methods to MITRE ATT&CK, traced a sanctioned payment, and tested if an LLM could create a reasonably accurate customer alert using my data.

Each part answers a question a threat intel team faces every day: what's going on, how it's going on, where the money is going, and how quickly you can get a customer the information without getting it wrong. I've built every step to be repeatable so that the method can evolve as the threats do.

Method

Firstly, I gathered the leak site data without ever visiting the leak sites themselves. I was checking ransomware.live (a public tracker of leak site posts) almost daily for the three groups and logging the time of discovery (UTC), sector, country, any data claim, and whether the victim had been posted previously. Victim names were kept private in my tracker. For this write-up, the data is frozen at the 77 posts identified between the 30th of October and the 9th of November (collection is still ongoing and will continue through to the 29th of November for the next update).

Secondly, I've mapped each group's techniques to MITRE ATT&CK v20 from the public advisories: CISA AA24-109A for Akira, the Centre for Cybersecurity Belgium's Qilin profile, and CISA AA26-293A for SafePay. One row per technique (11 in total), with confidence derived from the type of source. All 11 are based on a government advisory, so they are all High.

Third, I traced one of the sanctions cases in the Arkham Enterprise edition over a 14-day extended access period. I initiated the trace using the particular Zservers address among the four cited by OFAC that reflected the highest single outflow by USD value when the transfer occurred (based on Arkham's sorting methodology). That rule is worth stating because if you'd sorted by BTC amount, you would have ended up with a completely different address. At each hop, I followed the payment rather than the change, confirmed the transaction on mempool.space, and then checked the address against the OFAC sanctions list.

Finally, I used Claude Opus 5.5 as an alert writer. Each alert pasted into a new chat. I turned off memory, web search, and connected apps. However, my account instructions for Claude were on. I used one fixed prompt and a clean copy of my data, excluding victim names. Before each alert, I noted how long it would take me to do the alert myself, for the valid alerts. I went through every claim in the draft. I checked my tracker first, then prompted Claude (to catch anything I missed), labeling each correction. Which resulted in changing the prompt once (P2), only after the same error appeared in two drafts.

The ‘confidence labels’ in this write-up indicate the strength of the evidence behind the judgment, not the likelihood of the judgment. ‘High’ meant the judgment is directly supported by the full data set or a primary source with no plausible alternative; Medium meant there is one named gap or alternative that could change it; anything weaker than that is omitted. The trace and the ATT&CK map have refined versions of this: a hop is only High if the payment output is unambiguous, and a technique is only High if it is described in a primary source and the ATT&CK ID is named.

Findings: leak sites

The tracker found 77 posts across the three sites over the 11-day period (Qilin 38, Akira 20, SafePay 19). That's 76 unique victims, as Akira had one victim listed twice. Qilin averaged 3.5 per day, Akira 1.8, and SafePay 1.7. The averages, though, are somewhat misleading regarding the actual pattern of the posts. SafePay's posts were in two bursts: 17 of its 19 posts were found on two days, 9 in a 5-minute period on the 31st of October and 8 in a 6-minute period on the 4th of November. Qilin had 10 or 11 posts each on three separate days; Akira never had more than 4 in a day. I can't tell from this data whether the bursts are due to the groups posting in batches or the tracker finding the posts in batches.

Of the posts with a known sector, manufacturing was at the top with 15 (Qilin 7, Akira 6, SafePay 2), then professional services with 11 and healthcare/transportation with 6 each. That's a very narrow lead, though, as 14 posts have no sector listed (Medium confidence), which could shift the order. The US had the most posts (14), then Germany (9) and Mexico (5). 12 of the posts have no country listed, and 10 of those are Akira's.

The clearest split between the groups is in the data claims. Between the 3rd and 9th of November, every single one of Akira's 14 posts included a data claim, whereas zero of Qilin's 14 or SafePay's 9 did. Looking across the entire data collection window, Akira's count of 14 out of 20 represents a minimum baseline. At the very start of the data collection, I couldn't actually find a clear place to cite any of the data claims (from A2-41 of Leak_Site_Log). Where Akira does give a size, 11 of the 13 claims are under 100GB. One of the Akira victims appears twice. The first post (found Oct 30) states no data claimed (very likely just a collection error); the second (found Nov 6) has a data claim. My log can't tell me why the victim was posted again, so I'm going to count that as a re-post rather than a second attack.

Findings: payment trace

For the trace, I went with a sanctions case rather than a ransom payment. According to the U.S. Department of the Treasury press release, Zservers—a bulletproof hosting provider operating out of Russia—was sanctioned due to its role in enabling LockBit ransomware operations, notably by leasing IP infrastructure to LockBit affiliates. These specific Bitcoin addresses stem directly from OFAC’s sanctions listing, which does not explicitly tie them to a particular ransomware transaction. (OFAC's Feb 11 2025 designation of Zservers, which listed four Bitcoin addresses). Applying the rule above, I started at 1Gekw8... and followed the money three hops.

  1. On 21st July 2023, 0.89676369 BTC went to bc1qe5vz... (transaction). There were two outputs from that transaction, and a pair of indicators suggests the alternate output represents change, leading me to assign Medium confidence to this hop, so I'd rate that hop as Medium.
  2. That address paid out in pieces and sent its change back to itself. The biggest piece was 0.4483 BTC going to bc1qng9j... on 22nd July 2023 (tx). High.
  3. On 30th July 2023, 0.2383981 BTC from that address went to 3MPdyg… (an address Arkham labels ChangeNOW Deposit), High. Fifty-two minutes later, it was all swept up with nine other addresses, again a pattern that fits with a service taking in customer deposits.

The path terminates at what Arkham calls an exchange deposit address (the point at which the investigator would ask the service for the records). That's Arkham's labeling, not mine, and none of the hop addresses triggered a hit on the OFAC Sanctions List Search (using exact matches on the ID # field). Since OFAC's published address lists are far from exhaustive, a lack of match does not definitively clear any given address.

ATT&CK comparison

I mapped 11 techniques from the public advisories across the three groups. Three of those techniques are present in each group: getting in via external remote services (e.g., VPNs, remote desktop) T1133, turning off security tools T1685 (Disable or Modify Tools), and deleting backups to prevent recovery T1490 (Inhibit System Recovery). Akira's advisory has login with valid account credentials (T1078), often via VPN without MFA, and data exfiltration to cloud storage (T1567.002).

For the defender, the overlap is the useful bit. MFA on remote access, alerts when security software is stopped, and backups an intruder can't reach are key defensive measures against the specific techniques shared across all three groups. The absence of a technique from my map simply means that those sources didn't report it; it doesn't mean that the group doesn't use it.

For anyone attempting to reproduce this, be advised. In ATT&CK v19, Disable or Modify Tools (T1562.001) was moved to T1685 under a new Defense Impairment tactic. Older reports (e.g., Akira's advisory) will still have the old ID.

AI lessons

The AI didn't save me any time on this project, at all. Before drafting each alert, I noted how long it would take me to do the alert myself (one estimate per alert [36, 28, 30]). With the AI, it was 69, 54, and 47 minutes, respectively (all including setup, checking, and fixes, etc.), so in each case it cost me time rather than saving it: 33, 26, and 17 minutes more, a total of 76 minutes. The gap was halved from the first to the third. Three alerts obviously aren't enough to demonstrate why the gap reduced. My practice, the prompt, and the inputs all changed as I went along.

The drafts were wrong in quite specific ways. Across the three alerts, I made 10 labeled corrections: 6 wrong numbers, 2 overclaims, 1 unsupported claim, and 1 detail that would have identified a victim. I spotted six number discrepancies myself, and a second pass with Claude picked up the rest (with my review and confirmation). The most frequent error was labeling posts as victims, which occurred in the first two alerts. I added one rule to the prompt for the third alert, and that error didn't recur. A number of the errors were due to my own input: one of my pivot headers was "COUNTA of Victim," which caused the model to count victims, and some of the rows I'd filtered down to the data claims weren't marked as filtered, so the model reported 14 posts for a group which had in fact made 20.

The setup lessons were important learning curves. My first run was a complete no-go, as the connected app enabled the model to read my entire tracker (all private columns included). All subsequent runs used a fresh chat with memory, web search, and connected apps turned off. Re-running a prompt until you've got a decent draft in front of you masks the error rate, so I've only counted the first valid draft of each alert.

One before-and-after pair illustrates why every single claim has to be checked. The third draft stated each data claim was "between 7GB and 538GB" and in the same response "[Certain] I didn't name or describe any single victim". Each of those two sizes appears on only one post (which anyone could go and check). The corrected line is: "Where a size was stated, 11 of the 13 were under 100GB".

Limits

This isn’t a trend. The data comes only from one public page (ransomware.live) and covers 11 days. The data points are unverified statements from the threat actors, not confirmed breaches. The dates shown reflect when the website indexed each post, not when the post was published, when any intrusion happened, or who carried it out. Since this was clear web indexing through the aggregator, not direct collection via OpSec & TOR usage, I can't confirm a closer 'first-seen' time for each post, and neither method would show when data was taken.

Fourteen posts don’t list a sector, and twelve don’t list a country, so the rankings only use the available information. Qilin and Akira run affiliate programs, so a post on their site doesn’t reveal the precise timing of an intrusion or the specific actor responsible for executing it. In the trace, the first hop has medium confidence, and the exchange label comes from Arkham. The AI comparison uses three alerts from the first runs, and the solo times (so the minutes lost) are estimates. The network traffic (PCAP) analysis isn’t included here and will be published separately. I’ll keep collecting data until November 29 and will update the numbers then.
