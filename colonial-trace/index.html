OSINT Write-up: Tracing the Colonial Pipeline Ransom to the FBI Seizure

Nov 25, 2026 | Aaron M. Wood

The idea

I wanted to see whether I could reproduce a known blockchain-tracing case from start to finish using only free tools, and document it the way an investigator would have to. The Colonial Pipeline seizure is a good one to practice on because the answer is already public. In June 2021 the Department of Justice announced it had recovered 63.7 BTC of the DarkSide ransom, so I had a fixed endpoint to check my work against. My goal was to begin at the ransom payment and follow the money hop by hop, in a block explorer, all the way to the address the FBI seized from. The point was not to find anything new. It was to build a method I can repeat and defend: prove every link, validate every address, and know exactly where the chain stops telling me things.

Background

On May 7, 2021, the DarkSide ransomware group hit Colonial Pipeline and forced the shutdown of a major U.S. fuel pipeline. Colonial paid a ransom of about 75 BTC (roughly $4.4M at the time) the next day. On June 7, 2021, the DOJ announced it had seized 63.7 BTC (about $2.3M), most of the ransom, from a wallet the FBI held the private key to. The seizure warrant was signed by a U.S. Magistrate Judge in the Northern District of California.

DarkSide ran an affiliate program. The affiliate who carries out the intrusion and the developers who supply the malware split the proceeds, usually around 85/15. That split shows up on the blockchain, and being able to see it is part of what makes this case worth tracing.

Method

Firstly, I ran the whole trace in mempool.space, a free block explorer, so that anyone could reproduce it without a paid tool. I used ISACA's public write-up (Did the FBI Hack Bitcoin?) as a second reference to confirm I was on the right path, not as my source for the figures.

Secondly, I followed a few fixed rules at each hop. I followed the payment and not the change, so the small output that loops back to the sender was set aside. I required the output address I followed to show up as an input of the next transaction, because that address-to-address link is what makes the trace defensible instead of a guess. And I matched the amount from one hop to the next, down to the satoshi, so a wrong turn would show up as a figure that did not line up.

Thirdly, I validated every address before I trusted it. I wrote a small offline checker (Base58Check for the legacy and P2SH addresses, bech32 for the segwit ones) and ran all seven through it. A single wrong character almost always breaks the checksum, so this catches the kind of typo a truncated screenshot can hide. It caught one in my own notes.

Finally, every hop is written to a tracker row with the address, amount, date, transaction ID, a confidence label, and a note. Nothing in this write-up sits outside a row. The confidence labels work the same way throughout: a hop is High when the payment output and the address link are both unambiguous, and Medium when there is one named alternative that could change it.

The trace

Six hops carry the money from the ransom payment to the FBI's address. Every figure is read straight off the chain and confirmed in mempool.space. The list is numbered from hop 0, the ransom arriving, to match my tracker.

  1. The ransom. On May 8, 2021, about 75 BTC reached the DarkSide ransom address 15JFh88… as one output of a Coinbase batch payment (tx 6a798026). This is where the trace starts.
  2. 15JFh88… spent the funds on to 1DToN8Q… (75.00034246 BTC, tx 915fb4f0). High.
  3. On to bc1q7eqww… (74.99998307 BTC, tx fc78327d). The money is still whole here, with only a miner fee taken off. High.
  4. The split. The funds divide (tx 0677781a). 63.74998561 BTC goes to bc1qxu83… (the affiliate's share, about 85%) and 11.24962019 BTC goes to bc1qu57… (the developers' share, about 15%). I followed the affiliate's side. High.
  5. On May 9, 2021, bc1qxu83… paid 63.70000000 BTC to 3EYkx… (tx 9436dbf0) and sent a small change output back to itself. A flat, round 63.7 is a mild tell on its own, closer to a deliberate payout than an organic spend. High.
  6. Consolidation. On May 27, 2021, 3EYkx… fed a consolidation transaction (tx daf38c7b, input 13) that pooled a lot of addresses into the Subject Address bc1qq2euq8…, which then held 69.60422177 BTC. That total is worth a note. It mixes the 63.7 affiliate BTC with roughly 5.9 BTC from somewhere else, so not all of this wallet is Colonial money. The link is High, and the mixing is the reason the seized figure in the next hop is 63.7 and not 69.6.
  7. The seizure. On June 7, 2021, transaction 943f2d moved 63.69996546 BTC out of the Subject Address to an FBI-controlled address, bc1qpx7…, the 63.7 BTC the DOJ announced it had seized that day. High, confirmed against the DOJ release.

Findings

End to end, the chain holds. At every hop the output I followed turns up as the input of the next transaction, and each one is confirmed in mempool.space. A few things stand out. The affiliate model is visible, since the 85/15 split at hop 3 is DarkSide dividing its take in the open. The round 63.7 reads as a deliberate payout rather than an organic spend. And the seizure took the affiliate's 63.7, not the full 69.6 sitting in the consolidation wallet, which fits the DOJ seizing funds traceable to Colonial in particular rather than everything in the wallet. All seven addresses passed checksum validation.

The open question

The blockchain shows the 63.7 BTC moving out of the affiliate's wallet and into an FBI-controlled address. What it does not show is how the FBI got the key it needed to sign that transaction. The DOJ said the FBI "has the private key" to the address, but it did not say how it came by it. That is the question ISACA's piece digs into, and the useful part of the answer is what it rules out: this was not a break of Bitcoin's cryptography. A block explorer can document where the money went. It cannot show how a key was obtained. Knowing where that line sits is the part I would carry into a real case.

Limits

This is a solved, public case, so the value here is a method I can repeat, not a new finding. ISACA traced the path backward and did not give every hop's amount, so I worked each hop forward in mempool and logged the figures myself. The entity labels (the ransom address, the Subject Address, the FBI-controlled address) come from the DOJ record and ISACA's analysis, not from any attribution of my own. The 69.6 BTC in the consolidation includes funds that are not Colonial's, so only the 63.7 is cleanly the ransom. And a passing checksum proves an address is well formed, not that it is the right output of its transaction. That part is what the hop-to-hop address match establishes.
